## Origins

I grew up in a humble Norfolk farmhouse. It was nothing special—not listed—just red brick, oak beams, and, eventually, an AGA. The AGA represented something to me that I wasn’t able to articulate at the time. It was installed in the kitchen when I was eleven, around the same time I left the local primary school for the bright lights of a nearby prep school.

I was soon invited to my new school friends’ houses, and initiated into a world of country houses, rectories, manor houses, and deer parks. I loved every second of it, tiptoeing through room after room filled with antiques and smelling faintly of woodsmoke. These were historic, comfortable, and good-natured houses, and they were to me the epitome of… something I couldn’t put my finger on. Something I longed for.

These houses and their interiors were my first encounters with English country-house ‘style’, although it’s arguably more a feeling than a style. It is a way of decorating (or simply living in a space) that has held a particular fascination and appeal for me ever since. As I wrote this blog post, I finally realized why.

My friends’ houses differed in size and age, but the kitchen of every single one of them had an AGA (usually with several dogs sprawled in front of them). So, when I think back to the feeling I had when our own AGA was installed, I can finally articulate what it was that I perceived these houses and their inhabitants to have, and which I felt I lacked: the privilege of belonging.

## Privileged? Yes. Precious? No.

English country-house style, far from being as old as the houses it adorns, was forged in the early twentieth century by Nancy Lancaster and John Fowler, although its roots are found in the nineteenth-century ‘quest for comfort’ and the collecting tendencies of the aristocracy and gentry. Nowadays, it's probably better understood as ‘English style’, as its basic principles can be applied in the city as well as in the country, and in more humble buildings as well as in the country house. Having said this, English style does lend itself to grander interiors, but in an apparently nonchalant and unselfconscious way that suggests the inhabitants have almost forgotten their grandeur—after all, there are still horses to muck out and gardens to weed. Privileged? Yes. Precious? No.

The best description of this style that I have found is in Ben Pentreath’s book _English Decoration_. Imagine:

_A house in the country. On one façade… tall sash windows with slender glazing bars allow the afternoon light to stream into a faded drawing room._

_Softly patterned grey wallpaper is hung with prints and old oil paintings in dark gilt frames; here and there, the memory of a picture removed leaves a shadow on the wall…_

_From distant beech trees, a wood pigeon calls its song of summer. A bee drowsily meanders in through the open window and finds its way back out again… The wool carpet, the color of washed tobacco, moth-eaten at its perimeter, is overlaid with richly colored Turkey and Persian rugs, collected and laid down in another era._

_An ottoman is piled high with interesting books, and (…) a dog is fast asleep on an armchair. The scent of potpourri combines with the smell of woodsmoke from centuries of fires._

## A Paradoxical Recipe

Timelessness, comfort, connection with the outdoors. Not to mention a ubiquitous and informal mixture of art, antiques, and heirlooms alongside dog beds, paper lanterns, and holiday snaps. This is a style that is full of paradoxes: it is curated, yet casual; a bit disheveled, yet dignified; its influences and furnishings are global in origin, and yet it is a distinctly English look. The greatest paradox is captured in Nancy Lancaster’s observation that every room needs to have something ugly in it. Among contemporary designers, this is echoed in Rita Konig’s advice that ‘you do not need to love every piece’.

The more I think about it, the more I believe that the essence of English country-house style can’t be found in its parts; it must be taken as a whole. Does that make it elusive? Maybe. It is certainly not formulaic, although there seem to be some basic ingredients that can be combined for a fairly reliable result.

However, the final paradox is that the last stage of the recipe calls for the recipe book itself to be tossed away; English interiors, by definition, must mold themselves to the lives of those who occupy them. They are nothing if not lived in, not just by the present inhabitants but (seemingly) by generations of predecessors.

Writing this reminded me of Richard Long’s beautiful photograph _A Line Made by Walking_ (1967). Both this photograph and English country-house style interiors are defined by the fading trace of human activity, etched into its environment through repetition. In the English interior, this manifests as threadbare rugs, battered armchairs, and ‘the smell of woodsmoke from centuries of fires’.

## Style or Feeling?

But if the English country-house style can’t be defined by its constituent parts, it seems to me that it can be defined by the values and feelings that it evokes. The timeless quality of English interiors, for example, carries the reassuring notion of stability; the feeling that—at least in this house—nothing ever really changes. Wars may wage, economies may crash, pandemics may change the face of the earth; and yet, back at home, the fire still crackles in its grate and piles of books still teeter on window sills.

This style aims to communicate a feeling of integrity, its inherent grandeur tempered by a certain dog-eared character, which stops it from feeling pretentious. The house must be true to itself, never attempting to hide its flaws or deny the reality of the lives that are lived within it, be that muddy footprints through the kitchen or dog hair on the sofa. It is what it is, and the more of itself it is the better.

In fact, it seems to me that the more honest an interior is, the more comfortable it is, but in a larger sense than just a comfy seat and a warm fire. The kind of comfort that the best of these interiors can offer is the sense of a humble and tolerant acceptance of the whole of life—including you—warts and all. This kind of a welcome, from one imperfect friend to another, is like an invitation to relax in the knowledge that there’s no judgment here.

## The Privilege of Belonging

The greatest comfort of all, however, is surely the fact that this kind of interior is an expression of the life of its occupant; the occupant belongs there. The expression of self through the medium of ‘home’ is arguably one of the greatest aspirations of interior design, and this is nowhere better observed than in the English country-house style.

The feeling of belonging here is multi-dimensional. For instance, the real or perceived ancestral ties between the occupant and her possessions imply that she belongs to them as much as they belong to her. And, often, even the house itself ‘belongs’ to the landscape—either to the garden that has been created around it or to the land that bore the stones that form its walls.

There’s also a social dimension to the sense of belonging evoked by country-house style interiors, for obvious reasons; mainly, that the origins of this style lie with the landed upper classes. Until the early twentieth century, the landed classes were also the ruling elite—and no one belongs to the life of a country more than those who make the rules.

The English upper classes and their ancestral homes have, rightly or wrongly, become a widely recognized and nostalgic symbol for what it means to be English. Country-house style interiors, therefore, speak of a very particular kind of privilege: the privilege of belonging. They do not necessarily express the privilege of wealth, although that usually is the case. But the privilege of belonging can furnish you with something that no amount of money can buy: a healthy self-assurance. English country-house style exudes this self-assuredness, which is inevitably absorbed and felt by its inhabitants, even if—like my eleven-year-old self, sat with the dogs by the AGA—they do not quite belong to the world of the be-wellingtoned elite.
